Since going remote in 2016, I've been upgrading my office setup piece by piece each year. This year I moved and wanted to change the color of the desk to match the new office, and add a skirt to hide all the cords now that the desk sits in the middle of the room. I'd already stained and sealed the tabletop, so I figured it would be a quick project. I was wrong.

## The previous setup

Features on the desk and why each one is there:

- **Standing-desk base + oversized tabletop.** Stained and finished myself, because more space is always better. Lesson learned: there's a distance past which monitors are simply too far away.
- **Two monitor mounts.** Gets the monitors and computer off the desk and high enough to keep my neck neutral.
- **Keyboard and mouse pullout.** Keeps the desk clear and puts the keyboard at the right distance from the monitors. I couldn't find a monitor mount that would get the screens high enough, so lowering the keyboard won. Jury still out.
- **Upright mouse** (plus a left-hand one on the way). Micromovements were causing neck stiffness. Which then created the problem of where to put the second mouse. Another keyboard mount, obviously.
- **Split keyboard.** A whole other article. Love it, steep learning curve, three keyboards now living permanently on the desk.

## Stripping the wood

Harder than expected. I thought I could apply stripper, wait, scrape. In practice the stripper kept drying out before I got to it, so I covered the top in plastic drop cloth and pulled it back as I worked. Plastic stripping tools wore out fast; metal was the only thing that held up. Even then, the top coat was very labor intensive.

## Paint-washing the tabletop

I went with a paint wash instead of a stain. There were deep discolorations left over from the previous stain, and I figured the wash would help even out the grain with the lighter tone I wanted. It would also help blend the skirt with the tabletop, since the two woods were different.

<figure>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/images/house/work-desk-refresh/sanded.jpg' | relative_url }}" alt="Sanded tabletop with uneven grain tones">
  <figcaption>Stripped and sanded with 80 grit on a power sander. Note the uneven grain tones.</figcaption>
</figure>

I then applied AB wood bleach hoping to reduce the stain discoloration. The bleach didn't do much for the stain but did cut some of the red hues in the wood.

You may ask why the grain is so dark in places. Same reason as most of my projects: I forgot a step. I should write down what I'm planning to do, but I never do. This time I forgot to apply wood conditioner before staining last time around, which caused uneven absorption that I had to sand down before re-staining.

Next I mixed the wash. Roughly 10% paint, 90% water, with testers to dial in the color. I ended up combining two paints to get the tone I wanted, which nearly bit me later when I lost one of the cans before painting the skirt. Hand sanded the wash, wiped with a tack cloth.

Then a top coat. Water-based to avoid yellowing, but I still ended up with some yellow tones on the end grains. Not sure if that was the top coat itself or dust from sanding paint that I didn't clean off well enough. The top coat I used was [BEHR Gloss Clear Water-Based Spar Urethane](https://www.homedepot.com/p/BEHR-1-qt-Gloss-Clear-Water-Based-Interior-Exterior-Spar-Urethane-Wood-Sealer-B820404/311625413).

<figure>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/images/house/work-desk-refresh/top-coated.jpg' | relative_url }}" alt="Tabletop after top coat applied, smooth finish">
  <figcaption>Top coat applied.</figcaption>
</figure>

My usual approach on top coat: first two coats with a foam brush, hand sanding between each, wiping with a tack cloth or mineral spirits. Then a final coat wiped on with a rag. Tends to give a very smooth finish.

## Assembling the skirt

Below is a piece of skirt wood resting in front of the tabletop. Off to the right you can see the original size before the rip cut. To rip a board that long without a table saw, I used a circular saw and a [98" cutting guide](https://www.homedepot.com/p/Empire-98-in-Cutting-Guide-E902/203175575).

<figure>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/images/house/work-desk-refresh/ripcut.jpg' | relative_url }}" alt="Long board set up for rip cut with circular saw guide">
  <figcaption>Setting up the rip cut.</figcaption>
</figure>

**Important:** add a clamp in the middle of the cutting guide or you'll get a bowed cut. Getting everything measured and clamped so I could actually make the cut took far too much math and some trial and error.

Next, miter saw for length and 45-degree joints. I spent way too long trying to get clean miters with a circular saw, then gave up. We'd thrown out our old death-trap miter saw during the move, so after enough frustration I ordered a new one. The new saw gave perfect, precise cuts. Love that thing.

<figure>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/images/house/work-desk-refresh/miter-joints.jpg' | relative_url }}" alt="Mitered skirt joints assembled at corner">
  <figcaption>Mitered corner.</figcaption>
</figure>

I glued the miters: dowels for reinforcement, and wood glue makes a strong bond on its own. Best technique I found: lay the wood flat, tape the long ends with masking tape, apply glue, fold the joint up using a square to set 90 degrees. You can clamp with scrap above and below, but the tape tension was enough. The whole skirt was rigid 30 minutes after gluing.

<figure>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/images/house/work-desk-refresh/skirt-glued.jpg' | relative_url }}" alt="Skirt glued and taped at corners">
  <figcaption>Glued and taped.</figcaption>
</figure>

Then dowels. Drilled the skirt with a dowel jig, then measured and drilled the tabletop. The board I'd picked started straight but warped a bit after a few months in the humid garage. I debated swapping it out and am glad I didn't. The bend isn't noticeable and I think the dowels straightened it some.

<figure>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/images/house/work-desk-refresh/dowels.jpg' | relative_url }}" alt="Dowels drilled and ready for assembly">
  <figcaption>Dowels in.</figcaption>
</figure>

Then wood glue and **CLAMPS**. Would have used more if I had them.

Last steps: drilled holes for wires, mounted the two monitor mounts. At this point it was looking like another weekend, I didn't have the paint I needed, and I was ready to be done. Luckily a can of spray paint from a previous project was nearly a perfect match. I sprayed the skirt, the underside, and the yellow ends that had me debating about sanding the whole thing back to start over. Attached everything. Done.

The skirt is rough to the touch but I'm not sanding it. Done means done.

> **Update:** I ended up attaching the mouse mount to the left side and am getting rid of the right-hand mouse for now. About a week in and I'm adjusting.
